TWO KINDS OF CUSTOMER

Facebook finds the people
who already know you.

Think about where your last ten jobs came from. Most tradies will say a mix of two things: someone who already knew their name, and someone who didn't.

The people who know your name got it from a neighbour, a mate, a builder or a post in the local community group. Facebook is genuinely good at this. Your posts show up for people who follow you, your page has your number, and a recommendation in a suburb group can fill a week.

The people who don't know your name are standing in a flooded laundry or looking at a cracked retaining wall, phone in hand. They don't open Facebook. They type "plumber Parramatta" or "electrician near me" into Google. What comes up is the map pack, Google Business Profiles and websites that clearly say what the business does and where. A Facebook page is rarely what they land on, and even when it is, it may ask them to log in before they can see much.

So the real question isn't Facebook or a website. It's how much of your work you want from the second group. If the answer is "none, I'm flat out", you may not need a site. If the answer is "more", Facebook alone won't get you there.

THE 5-MINUTE TEST

Find out before
you spend a cent.

Do this on your phone, logged out of Facebook if you can (a private browser tab works), because that's how a stranger sees you:

  1. Search your trade and your main suburb. Write down the first five businesses you see. How many have a website? Are you on the first screen at all?
  2. Search your own business name. Does your Facebook page come up, a directory listing, or someone else with a similar name?
  3. Tap through to your Facebook page as a stranger. Can you see your services, the suburbs you cover and recent jobs without logging in or scrolling through months of posts?
  4. Check your diary. Over the next month, how many days are booked, and how many are you hoping to fill?
  5. Ask your last few new customers how they found you. Not regulars, new ones. Word of mouth, Facebook, or Google?

If your diary is full, every new customer came from a referral, and you're happy that way: keep the Facebook page, set up a Google Business Profile, and spend your money elsewhere. If the businesses ranking above you all have websites, or your new customers are mostly finding you through Google, the gap is worth closing.

WHAT YOU OWN, WHAT YOU RENT

Your Facebook page
lives on Meta's land.

A Facebook page costs nothing and it's quick to set up. The trade-off is that the page, the posts, the reviews and the followers all sit on Meta's platform under Meta's terms. If your account gets hacked, restricted or disabled, all of that can be out of reach until Meta sorts it out, and getting through to Meta isn't easy. A website on a domain registered in your name is an asset you hold, whatever happens to any social account.

  • Facebook decides which followers see each post, not you
  • A Facebook page gives Google little to read about your jobs and suburbs
  • A website can be found by people who have never heard of you
  • Your domain and your site stay yours if a social account goes down
  • A site is somewhere your Google Business Profile can point that answers the caller's questions on one screen

QUESTIONS TRADIES ASK

Straight answers.

Is a Facebook page enough for a tradie?

It can be, if nearly all your work comes from referrals, repeat customers and local community groups, and your diary is already full. It's not enough if you want work from people who have never heard of you and search Google for your trade and suburb, because Google's local results lean on your Google Business Profile and a website, not on Facebook posts.

Can I just link my Google Business Profile to my Facebook page?

You can, and it's better than nothing. But a Facebook page gives Google very little to read about which jobs you do and which suburbs you cover, and a customer tapping through from Google lands in an app that may ask them to log in. A simple website answers those questions on one page with a button to ring you.

Should I stop posting on Facebook if I get a website?

No. Keep posting if it brings you work. The website is the home base that Google, your Business Profile and your Facebook page all point to. Your job photos from Facebook can go straight onto the site.

What happens if I lose access to my Facebook page?

Your page, posts, reviews and followers sit on Meta's platform under Meta's rules, so a hacked or disabled account can take them out of reach until Meta restores it. A website on a domain registered in your name stays yours whatever happens to a social account.
