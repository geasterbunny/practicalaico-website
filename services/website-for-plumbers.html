TWO CUSTOMERS, ONE SITE

The emergency caller
and the careful planner.

Plenty of plumbing websites are built for neither. They open with a big stock photo of a smiling bloke holding a wrench, a paragraph about "quality service since forever", and a phone number buried in the footer. The emergency caller scrolls twice, gives up and taps the next result. The planner can't find a single photo of a real job, so they go with whoever looked more legit.

What the emergency caller needs

Speed and certainty. Is this a plumber? Do they come to my suburb? Can I ring them right now? That's it. A tap-to-call button at the top of the screen, the words "blocked drains, burst pipes, hot water" where they can see them, and an honest line about your hours. If you do after-hours call-outs, say which jobs and which hours. If you don't, say so, and give them the form so you can ring back first thing.

What the planner needs

Reasons to trust you with a job worth thousands. A hot water system swap, a bathroom rough-in, a gas cooktop connection, re-piping an old house. They want photos of work you've actually done, your licence details, the areas you cover, and a sense of how you price. They'll read your about page. They'll check your Google reviews. They might fill in a quote form at 9pm on a Sunday, so the form needs to be short and needs a quick reply.

The trick is that these two sit on the same home page. The top of the screen is for the emergency. Everything below it is for the planner.

THE PLUMBER'S CHECKLIST

Check your current site
against this tonight.

Open your site on your phone, not the computer, and go through this list. It doesn't matter who built it or who builds the next one. If you can tick all of these, you probably don't need us.

  1. The call button is visible without scrolling, and tapping it actually dials.
  2. Your job types are named in customers' words. "Blocked drain", "no hot water", "leaking tap", "gas leak". Not "comprehensive plumbing solutions".
  3. Your service area is written out as suburbs, not "Greater Sydney and surrounds". If you don't go past a certain point, say so. It saves you pointless calls.
  4. Your hours are honest. If the site says 24/7, someone answers at 2am. If not, it says when you do answer.
  5. Your licence details are shown, including the right category for gasfitting or drainage if you do it.
  6. The photos are yours. A tidy copper manifold or a new hot water unit you installed beats any stock image.
  7. There's a short quote form for planned work: name, number, suburb, what the job is, a photo upload if possible.
  8. It loads quickly on mobile data. Try it with wi-fi off.
  9. Your Google Business Profile links to it, with the same name, areas and hours on both.
  10. The domain is registered in your name, not the builder's.

About the licence line

This one isn't optional in NSW. Government guidance on building trade advertising says advertising for specialist work, which includes plumbing, draining and gasfitting, must show the licensee's name (or registered business name) and the licence number for the right category of work, and that covers the internet. Other states and territories run their own licensing, so check your regulator. Either way, a visible licence number is one of the fastest ways to tell a nervous customer you're the real thing.

Source: NSW Government, building trade advertisements, checked November 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.

QUESTIONS PLUMBERS ASK

Straight answers.

What should a plumber's website have on the home page?

A tap-to-call button and a short job form you can see on a phone without scrolling, the jobs you do in plain words (blocked drains, hot water, leaks, gas), the suburbs you cover, your licence details, and photos of your own work. Everything else is secondary.

Do I have to show my plumbing licence number on my website?

In NSW, yes. Government guidance on building trade advertising says ads for specialist work such as plumbing, draining and gasfitting, including on the internet, must show the licensee's name (or registered business name) and the licence number for the right category of work. Other states have their own rules, so check with your regulator.

Should I say I'm a 24/7 emergency plumber on my site?

Only if someone really answers at 2am. If you do after-hours work, say exactly when and which jobs. If you don't, say your hours clearly. A customer who rings an advertised emergency line and gets voicemail may well leave a review about it.

How much does a website for a plumber cost?

Our Website Build is from A$1,490 fixed for five pages, with the domain in your name, the code handed over and hosting charged at cost. You get a fixed price within two business days of one email, before anything starts.

Can I get one page for each suburb I service?

You can, but dozens of near-identical suburb pages with the name swapped tend to help nobody. A clear service areas section listing the suburbs you actually cover, plus a Google Business Profile with the same areas, is the honest starting point. Add a suburb page only when you have something real to say about work there.
