THE PLUMBER'S PHONE PROBLEM

Two kinds of call.
One phone in your pocket.

Plumbing calls split into two piles. One pile is planned work: a hot water swap next week, a quote for a bathroom, a blocked drain that can wait until morning. The other pile is the burst flexi hose, the sewer overflowing into the yard, the gas smell. The first pile wants a booking. The second pile wants you, now.

The trouble is that both piles ring while you're under a house or elbow-deep in a trench. If you answer everything, you never finish a job. If you answer nothing, the burst pipe rings the next plumber on Google, and the quote request goes to whoever called back first.

A basic answering service takes a message from both piles and leaves you to sort them later. That's the bit that matters for a plumber: the sorting. A receptionist is only useful to you if it sorts the way you would.

Count your own two piles first

Before you spend anything, check whether this is your problem. Take ten minutes with your call log:

  1. Go back two weeks and list every missed call from a number not in your contacts.
  2. Next to each one, write whether it turned out to be an emergency, routine work, or junk.
  3. Mark the ones you didn't ring back within an hour.
  4. Put a rough job value next to the emergencies and the routine work you lost.

If you miss one or two calls a fortnight, a callback habit is cheaper than any service, ours included. If you're missing after-hours emergencies, or losing a steady trickle of quote requests while you're on the tools, keep reading.

USEFUL WHETHER YOU BUY OR NOT

Write your triage rulebook
before anyone answers for you.

Whoever picks up your phone, a family member, a message service or an AI, needs the same thing: a short written rulebook. Most plumbers carry it in their head. Writing it down is the step that makes any of these options work. Here's a starting template. Change every line to suit how you run.

1. The questions to ask every caller

  • Name, mobile and the address of the job, including the suburb.
  • Is water running right now, or has it stopped?
  • Have you turned off the water main? Do you know where it is?
  • Is water near any power points, lights or the switchboard?
  • Is it the hot or the cold side, or the hot water system itself?
  • Is it a toilet or sewer problem, and is it the only toilet in the house?
  • Can you smell gas?
  • Is it a house, a unit, or a commercial property? Owner or tenant?

2. What counts as an emergency (sent to you, any hour)

  • Water still running that the caller can't stop at the main.
  • Water near electrics.
  • Sewage overflowing inside, or the only toilet blocked.
  • Any mention of a gas smell. Your script tells the caller what to do while they wait.

3. What can be booked without you

  • Quotes and site inspections, inside your business hours and service area.
  • Routine service: leaking taps, running toilets, hot water checks, drain clearing that isn't overflowing.
  • Follow-up visits for existing customers.

4. What to tell the caller while they wait

Most plumbers want a caller with a leak told to turn off the water main, which is often near the water meter at the front boundary, and to open a tap to drain the pipes. RACV gives the same first step: turn off the water mains to limit further damage. Whatever you want said, write it word for word. The receptionist reads your approved script, not its own advice.

QUESTIONS PLUMBERS ASK

Straight answers.

Can an AI receptionist tell a burst pipe from a dripping tap?

It asks the questions you give it, such as whether water is still running, whether the caller has found the main, and whether water is near power. Your answers to those questions decide what happens next. You write the triage rules; it follows them and texts you a summary after every call.

Will it book emergency call-outs at night without asking me?

Only if you tell it to. Most plumbers set it so after-hours emergencies come straight to them as a summary, and only routine work such as quotes, hot water service checks and blocked drains during business hours gets booked into approved slots.

Can it tell the caller how to turn off their water?

Yes, if you approve the wording. Most plumbers want callers told to find the water main, usually near the meter at the front boundary, and turn it off while they wait. It reads the script you sign off and nothing more.
