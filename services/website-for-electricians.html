WORK NOBODY CAN SEE

Your work ends up
behind plasterboard.

A bricklayer's work is on show for decades. Yours disappears into the wall the day the plasterer turns up. The customer is left judging you on everything except the work itself: did you look legitimate, did you answer, did you turn up.

That makes an electrician's website a different job from most trade sites. It isn't a gallery. It's a place where a stranger checks, quickly, that you're licensed, that you do their kind of job, and that you come to their suburb.

And "their kind of job" splits in two.

The homeowner

The safety switch keeps tripping. Half the house has no power. They want two more power points in the kitchen, a ceiling fan before summer, or an EV charger in the garage. They search on a phone, they're a bit nervous about electricity, and they compare two or three sparkies in a couple of minutes. They want a name, a licence, a suburb and a number.

The commercial client

A builder pricing a shop fit-out, a strata manager with a faulty exit light, a facilities manager who needs testing and tagging done across three sites. They aren't rushed and they aren't nervous. They're ticking boxes: the right licence, current insurance, similar jobs done before, someone who answers email. Often they've been given your name by someone else and are just checking you're real.

Put both on one muddled services list, with ceiling fans next to switchboard upgrades for a warehouse, and each reader assumes you mostly do the other kind of work.

THE TEN-MINUTE TEST

Check your site on your
phone before you spend a cent.

This works whoever built your site and whoever builds the next one. Open it on your phone with the wi-fi off. If you can tick every line, you probably don't need us.

  1. Your licence details are on every page, not hidden on the about page. The footer is fine.
  2. The call button shows without scrolling, and tapping it dials.
  3. A homeowner and a builder can each find their section from the home page in one tap.
  4. Jobs are named the way people search. "Safety switch keeps tripping", "EV charger installation", "smoke alarms". Not "complete electrical solutions".
  5. Your suburbs are written out. If you don't cross the bridge, say so.
  6. Specialist tickets are named. If you're a Level 2 accredited service provider in NSW, or accredited for solar and batteries, say so in those words. People search for them.
  7. Your hours are true. Only say you do after-hours call-outs if someone picks up after hours.
  8. The photos are yours. A neat switchboard you wired beats a stock photo of a hard hat.
  9. It says what the customer is left with. In NSW a customer can be given a copy of the Certificate of Compliance for Electrical Work. If you send one, say so. It's a trust signal most sites skip.
  10. Your Google Business Profile matches the site: same name, same suburbs, same hours.
  11. The domain is registered in your name, not the builder's.

About the licence line

In NSW this isn't a nice-to-have. Government guidance on building trade advertising says the rules cover the internet, that electrical wiring work of any value can't be advertised without a current contractor licence, and that advertising must show the licensee's name (or registered business name) and the licence number with the correct category of work. Other states and territories run their own licensing, so check with your regulator.

LOCAL SEARCH, WITHOUT THE MYSTERY

Found for the job,
in the suburb.

People tend to search for the job and the place: "switchboard upgrade Marrickville", "electrician near me", "EV charger install Northern Beaches". Google answers those with a map and a handful of local businesses. Your Google Business Profile is what puts you on that map, and it helps when your website says the same things the profile does.

So the work is plain and a bit dull. The profile is filled in properly, with the right categories and service areas. The site names the same jobs and the same suburbs in ordinary words. Each main job type has a section a customer could land on and understand without reading anything else. The site is quick on mobile data and readable by Google.

What we don't do is churn out forty suburb pages with the name swapped. They read as filler to customers and they're a chore to maintain. One honest service-area section does more.

Nobody can promise you a spot on the map. Anyone who does is guessing. What you can control is whether Google has a clear, consistent picture of what you do and where.

QUESTIONS ELECTRICIANS ASK

Straight answers.

What should an electrician's website include?

A tap-to-call button and a short job form at the top of the phone screen, your jobs named in the words customers use (power points, switchboard upgrades, safety switches, lighting, EV chargers), the suburbs you cover, your licence details, and photos of your own work. If you do commercial work, add a separate section with your capabilities, insurance and past projects.

Do I have to show my electrical licence number on my website?

In NSW, yes. Government guidance on building trade advertising says advertising for electrical wiring work, including on the internet, must show the licensee's name (or registered business name) and the licence number with the correct category of work. Other states and territories have their own rules, so check with your regulator.

Should residential and commercial electrical work be on the same website?

Usually yes, on one site with two clear paths. Homeowners and commercial clients look for different things, so the home page should send each to their own section rather than mixing ceiling fans and fit-outs in one list. If almost all your work is one type, build the site around that and give the other a short mention.
